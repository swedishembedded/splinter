\section{Discussion and limits}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What the process achieves.} Supervised training on about two thousand verified trajectories accounts for
the step that the evidence supports firmly: the 4B and 8B models go from no solved exam to \examFourFullFive{} and
\examEightFullFive{} of 100 tasks they have never seen, and they resend a failed call less often than their base
models (\behaviourFourFullExamRepeatPct{}\% against \behaviourFourBaseExamRepeatPct{}\% of calls after an error at 4B,
\behaviourEightFullExamRepeatPct{}\% against \behaviourEightBaseExamRepeatPct{}\% at 8B). The later stages are less clear. The second round of data and the policy-gradient stage each gave a higher
count at 8B in one run (\examEightRtwoFive{} and \examEightPgFive{}), one of them not significant and the other
nominally so without correction, and neither gave a gain at 4B. The teacher's continuations do what they were built
for as data, in that \recoverySolvedPct{}\% of them end in a verified solution that follows the student's own mistake,
but their effect on the student was not isolated.

\paragraph{Where it stops.} The 0.6B model improves on the training tasks (\trainZeroFullPct{}\% of attempts pass) and
solves \examZeroFullFive{} of 100 exams, so this tool and this data do not bring a model of that size to the exams.
At 4B and 8B the watchdog still ends about a third of the exam attempts, and the penalty we tried did not change that,
although the optimiser moved the policy and the long, looping attempts were in its data. One possible reason, which we
did not test, is that the reward sees little of the behaviour it penalises: the adapters loop in
\behaviourFourFullTrainLoopPct{}\% and \behaviourEightFullTrainLoopPct{}\% of their attempts at the training tasks,
which is where the policy gradient gets its attempts, against \behaviourFourFullExamLoopPct{}\% and
\behaviourEightFullExamLoopPct{}\% at the exams.

\paragraph{Limits.} Each arm has one seed. The round-one recipe, measured \roundOneFourRuns{} times at 4B and
\roundOneEightRuns{} times at 8B, solved \roundOneFourMin{} to \roundOneFourMax{} and \roundOneEightMin{} to
\roundOneEightMax{} tasks within five attempts, and pass@1 moved by up to \replicateEightOneDiff{} tasks between two
adapters of the same recipe; the 8B gains of the later stages are single measurements and have to be read against that
spread. The $p$ values are not corrected for the number of comparisons. The 0.6B round-two adapter was measured with five attempts of every task, against the base model of its own run, instead of reruns of the failed tasks. No
arm removes one kind of record, and no arm runs the policy gradient without the loop penalty, so neither the recovery
data nor the penalty is isolated. The exams are 100 tasks of one domain, and a count on them carries a wide interval
(\examFourFullFiveLo--\examFourFullFiveHi{}\% for the 4B round-one adapter). The repeat-after-error share is measured
on first attempts only, and looping is measured through the watchdog's stopping rule.

Three qualifications of the protocol of \cref{sec:task} apply. The base-model arms left \uncountedFourBase{} (4B) and
\uncountedEightBase{} (8B) slots uncounted after retries; the base models solved nothing in the slots that counted.
The 0.6B round-one adapter left \uncountedZeroFull{} slot uncounted and so misses the validity rule; its count could
be higher by at most one task. Every other adapter arm counted every slot. The policy-gradient recipe
was changed once after an exam result: with training rows bounded at 8\,192 tokens, the stage produced an adapter with
more loop-stopped attempts than the adapter it started from, and that bound had removed the long, looping attempts from
its data. The reported runs use the bound of 16\,384 tokens. Finally, the teacher is a larger model of the students'
family and the records are only as good as its verified solutions: nothing here shows how the process behaves when no
stronger model is available.
