\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A person's writing carries more than facts and style. It carries the person's judgment:
what they weigh against what, what they refuse to do whatever the circumstances, and which
consideration yields when two collide. A language model fine-tuned on such writing could
decide a new matter as the writer would, and, as an agent, carry the decision out through the
tools it is given, including tools it was not trained with. This paper measures whether
fine-tuning on one author's writings produces that behaviour, in a setting where the answer
can be checked mechanically.

Measuring it on a real author is hard for two reasons. The model may have met the author in
pretraining, so a correct answer does not show that anything was learned from the training
texts. And what the author would decide in a new situation is open to interpretation, so
there is no answer key that does not itself rest on judgment, whether a rater's or a judge
model's. Evaluations of models that take on a character or a population use retained
knowledge of the character or survey answers as the reference (\cref{sec:related}); neither
is a decision graded against a ground truth that pretraining cannot have contained.

We remove both problems with a synthetic author. An author is a pure function of a seed: it
holds four beliefs from a fixed set of six tenets, each read one of two opposed ways, in a
hidden order of priority. Given a situation, code computes what such an author decides and
which belief decided it. Situations are set in places, cases and outside houses whose names
are made from syllables drawn from the seed, with invented years and household counts. The
author's essays are written by a language model that is given the beliefs privately and the
decision of each case, and is instructed to let the beliefs show only in what is decided,
never stating them as rules. A model trained on decisions read out of these essays is then
examined on situations whose topics, combinations of facts and options, and tool schemas
were withheld from the training data, and each answer is graded by comparing the option named
in a tool call with the oracle's decision.

\paragraph{Question and thesis.}
The research question is whether supervised fine-tuning on decisions read out of one author's
essays makes a model take that author's decision, through a tool call, on cases whose topic,
combination of facts and options, or tool schema the training data does not contain, and
whether it does so more often than the same model given the author's identity, the author's
beliefs or the author's own text in the prompt. Our thesis is that, under this controlled
setting, a LoRA adapter trained on such decisions takes the oracle's decision far more often
than the base model told the author's name or given the author's beliefs as stated
principles, on withheld topics, combinations and tools alike. The thesis would be contradicted
if the adapter did not exceed these two prompted arms in the paired comparison, or if its
accuracy on the withheld cells fell to the level of the prompted arms. Retrieval of the
author's essay paragraphs at answer time is the strongest competing approach we test; whether
the adapter exceeds it is reported per author and is not part of the thesis, because it holds
for two of the three authors only.

\paragraph{Scope.}
This paper concerns fine-tuning a language model on one author's writings so that it
decides, and acts through tool calls, as the author would on situations, topics and tools it
has not been trained on; the pipeline that turns the writings into an adapter; and the design
of the evaluation (an oracle with known hidden beliefs, held-out axes, mechanical grading,
paired statistics, and baselines that put the author's name, the author's beliefs or retrieved
passages of the writings in the prompt). It reports measurements on three synthetic authors
with one policy model, Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3}. The authors' writings are
model-written; style, voice and the reasons a model gives are not measured.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled benchmark of synthetic authors with a code oracle, hold-out of topics, of
  combinations of facts and options, and of tool schemas, graded by string comparison with no
  judge model (\cref{sec:benchmark}), and the pipeline from essays to adapter that it
  evaluates (\cref{sec:training}), shown on one case from beginning to end with the real data
  of every stage (\cref{sec:walkthrough}).
\item Measurements on three authors for five arms on one fixed examination each, with exact
  paired tests and Wilson intervals, the adapter's errors, a comparison of what adapters
  trained on different authors decide where those authors disagree, and an audit of the
  training data against the oracle (\cref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

The adapter's accuracy on the examinations is \AccATuned, \AccBTuned\ and \AccCTuned\ for the
three authors, against \AccABase, \AccBBase\ and \AccCBase\ for the unadapted model; a uniform
guess is right one time in three, because every item offers three options. Retrieval of essay
paragraphs is a strong baseline (\AccARetrieval, \AccBRetrieval\ and \AccCRetrieval), and the
adapter's lead over it is not significant for one of the three authors. \Cref{sec:discussion}
states what these results establish and the limits of their validity.
