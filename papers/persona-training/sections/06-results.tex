\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Every exam in this section is \textbf{pilot scale}: 31 to 42 tasks over 6 or 7 families,
run with the earlier exam of \cref{sec:exam-versions}. Their families are fixed by each
run's held-out split before the candidate is trained, but they are far too small to carry a
claim, and they were reused: two exams share a 35-task set (A), three share a 31-task set
(B), and the Adams exam has its own (C), so the six comparisons are not independent and the
recipes were developed while looking at them. None is a frozen final test.

\subsection{Candidates}

\Cref{tab:training} lists the training runs. The runs differ in more than the data (the
recipe changed between them, \cref{sec:training-defects}); we do not claim that any difference in
outcome is due to a single change. Held-out loss and token accuracy fall in every run, but token
accuracy is nearly flat (for v7 it moves from 0.731 to 0.750), which is plausible for a
mainly stylistic change by analogy with~\citet{lin2024urial}, so that measure says little
about voice. Losses are not comparable across runs with different held-out sets.

\paragraph{The persona prompt.}
The base and prompted-base arms are the same in every exam of a task set, so the prompt's
effect is read once per set (\cref{tab:prompt}). On both Jefferson sets the prompt raised
the base from 9 to 14 of 35 and from 8 to 13 of 31 tasks judged right, and cut the answers
with invented specifics from 20 to 3 and from 21 to 6. On the Adams set it did not raise the
count judged right (10 against 9) and halved the invented answers (16 to 8). The prompted
arm was not paired with the base arm in a stored test; the marginals bound the task-level
sign test. Whatever the split of discordant tasks, the fall in invented answers has
one-sided $p\leq0.003$ on both Jefferson sets, ignoring family clustering, which with seven
families can make these values too small. The gain in tasks judged right has $p$ between
0.031 and 0.20 on each Jefferson set, so it is not resolved.

\paragraph{The candidates against the prompted base.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No candidate separates from the prompted base.} The family-level test has
  $p\geq0.109$ in every exam. The candidates are judged right on 0 to 8 more tasks than the
  prompted base (v6 0, ablation 4, v7 1, rehearsal 1, e4 3, adams 8). The bounds of
  \cref{tab:bounds} show that, ignoring clustering, only the Adams exam could have reached
  $p<0.05$ at the task level, and only under some splits.
\item \textbf{The voice ablation.} v6 and ablation share set A and the dialogue data;
  ablation adds the 210 fixed-prompt voice rows (and, with the larger dataset, 81 rather
  than 54 steps). It is judged right on 18 against 14 for the prompted base and 14 for v6,
  with five wins in six discordant families against the prompted base ($p=0.109$); at the
  task level $p$ lies between 0.062 and 0.30 whatever the split. This is not evidence of an
  effect of the voice rows.
\item \textbf{Against the unprompted base.} The ablation candidate won all five of its
  discordant families ($p=0.031$, one-sided, five units), the only family-level result below
  0.05 in the table and one of twelve family-level tests in it, uncorrected for
  multiplicity. The comparison changes both the prompt and the weights, so it does not
  separate what each contributes.
\item \textbf{Adams.} 17 against 9 right with the prompted base (net $+8$ tasks) and 6 against
  8 invented. At the task level the one-sided $p$ could lie anywhere from 0.004 to 0.084;
  the family-level test, with three of six families discordant, gives 0.125.
\item \textbf{Invented specifics, candidate against prompted base.} The candidates state
  invented specifics in 1 to 3 fewer answers than the prompted base in five exams and in 1
  more in v6; the smallest possible task-level $p$ of any of these is 0.125.
\item \textbf{Rehearsal and the current recipe.} The rehearsal candidate judged 14 of 31
  right against 13 for the prompted base; e4, the only candidate of the current recipe with
  an exam, 16 against 13, with 3 invented against 6. Neither is distinguishable (family-level
  $p=0.5$ and $0.125$). The e4 exam was judged by the greedy judge and the others on set B by
  the sampled one, so its comparison with v7 and rehearsal also changes the judge.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reading.}
The exams are consistent with candidates a few points better than the prompted base and
with candidates no better than it. We therefore do \emph{not} claim that fine-tuning beats
prompting for this task. What the data support is narrower: on the Jefferson task sets a
persona prompt alone markedly reduces answers with specifics absent from the source; no
candidate is judged right on fewer tasks than the prompted base of its exam; and none is
distinguishable from it.

\subsection{Release gate}

No Qwen3 candidate has been released. The Jefferson v7 candidate passed the improvement check
(on the suite of \cref{sec:defects}, mostly paraphrases of trained tasks: 33 item wins against
3, 12 of 12 discordant families) and failed the anchor check (accuracy 0.773 against 0.800,
drop 0.027 against a bound of 0.02, \cref{tab:anchor}); its serve check was not measured
because the serving binary was absent. The Adams candidate passed improvement (54 item wins
against 9 over 207 tasks, 165 of them variants; 13 of 13 discordant families), failed the
anchor (0.764 against 0.800) and had no serve measurement for the same reason. The
rehearsal candidate passed the anchor (0.818 against 0.800, 9 candidate-only against 7
base-only items) but failed improvement (29 item wins against 7, but 8 of 11 discordant
families, $p=0.113$) and the serve check: the server answered all eight sampled tasks, the
judge left every served answer ungraded, and the check as then written counted an
ungraded answer as a disagreement (\cref{sec:residency}). The anchor result of one
rehearsal run does not show that rehearsal protects general behaviour: under no change the
anchor drop has a standard deviation of about 0.037 (\cref{tab:anchor}). The only release
the gate has made was for an earlier student model (a DeepSeek-R1 distilled 7B), whose
served model re-answered 8 of 8 sampled tasks alike.

\subsection{The frozen exam}\label{sec:frozen}

The final test and dev suite were reserved from the pool of letters left out of the materials,
before any task was generated, with 50 families each. Generation admitted tasks from only 39
families of the final test (244 tasks: 22 families with the full eight, the others with one
to seven, mean 6.3) and from 44 of the dev suite (166 tasks), in the three kinds
conversation, advice and explanation. The planned $50\times8$ design (400 tasks) was
therefore not reached; at the realised size the simulated power for a ten-point gain is
0.55 under the planning assumptions (\cref{tab:power}). The first 25 families by name, the
pilot subset, hold 155 tasks.

\subsection{Cost}

Reported to explain the design, not as performance claims. Generating tasks over 19 of
1,629 parts took 6,477~s; teaching, verification, authoring, variants and selection for the
v7 run took about five hours together (257 teacher solves: 161 verified, 95 failed, one
undecided); training v7 took about two hours for 56 steps; an exam of 31 tasks over three
arms with judging took about 37 minutes; a release gate over 259 tasks and two arms took
about two hours and 12 minutes. At the observed pace, covering all 1,629 parts with
generated tasks would take 555,322~s (6.4 days), which is why the voice view, which needs no
model, carries most of the author's text.
