\begin{abstract}
We describe and evaluate an autonomous pipeline that takes one natural-language
sentence (``Learn to think like \ldots\ based on the materials \ldots\ in directory
\ldots'') and a directory of an author's writings, and produces a LoRA-adapted
language model. The policy is Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode, a separate Qwen3-14B
is the judge, and all work runs on two 24~GB Tesla P40 GPUs. The pipeline plans,
captures sources, splits them by \emph{family} (all printings of a text), generates and
verifies tasks, teaches, builds a dataset that mixes verified dialogues with the author's
own text, fine-tunes, examines the result on held-out families, and applies a release gate.
Thomas Jefferson and Samuel Adams serve as test subjects.

The paper is mainly about method: how such a system is measured, and the defects that
were found in it. We report a split rule that survives overlapping editions, an exam
design whose primary comparison is the base model under the same persona prompt and whose
size is chosen by simulation, controls on a model judge, and defects found by audit: a
release gate whose ``held-out'' improvement was mostly paraphrases of trained tasks, a
retention check whose bound lay inside its own noise and whose grader failed correct long
answers, a monitoring split that silently dropped 27\% of the author's voice records, and
training-loop mechanics (per-record loss normalisation, a learning rate that never
annealed under early stopping, sampling with replacement).

The results are pilot scale and do not establish an effect of fine-tuning. On six exams
over three task sets (31--42 tasks, 6--7 families each) no candidate is distinguishable from
the base model given the persona in its prompt: the family-level sign test against that arm
has $p\geq0.109$ in every exam, and with at most seven families it could reach $\alpha=0.05$
only through a clean sweep of at least five discordant families. The candidates were judged
right on 0 to 8 more tasks than the prompted base. On the two Jefferson task sets the persona
prompt alone cut the number of answers stating specifics absent from the source from 20 to 3
and from 21 to 6. A 39-family, 244-task exam reserved before any generation has a simulated
power of about 0.55 for a ten-point gain; no run on it completed, and no result on it is
reported.
\end{abstract}
