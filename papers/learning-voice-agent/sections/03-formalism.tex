\section{Teachings: a formal account}\label{sec:teachings}

Definitions follow the implementation (\code{core::teaching}); each statement is the specification of a test in that module.

\paragraph{Teachings.}
A teaching is one of: a \emph{fact} $(\sigma,c,\kappa)$, a subject $\sigma$, the claim $c$ as the user stated it, and whether it corrects an
earlier statement $\kappa$; a \emph{rule} $(i,f)$, an instruction $i$ and a force $f\in\{\textsc{Always},\textsc{Never}\}$; or a \emph{procedure}
$(n,s_1,\ldots,s_m)$ with a name and $m\ge2$ ordered steps. Nothing else is a teaching, and nothing in a teaching changes a model's weights.

\paragraph{The plain reader.}
A transcript $u$ (case ignored, apostrophes normalised) is a teaching only if it is not a question and begins with an opener. Rule openers (``from now
on'', ``next time'', ``going forward'', \ldots) bind what follows; a bare ``always'' or ``never'' binds only when at least three words follow and the
first is not a filler such as ``a'' or ``mind'' (so ``Never mind'' and ``Always a pleasure'' are not rules). A rule whose instruction begins with a
subject other than the listener (``I'', ``we'', ``they'', \ldots) is not a rule: ``Next time I will bring coffee'' says what someone else will do. Fact openers
are of two kinds: ones that ask to remember (``remember that'', ``note that'', \ldots) and ones that correct (``actually'', ``that is wrong'', \ldots); the rest
must split at its first joining verb (``is'', ``are'', \ldots) into a subject of one to six words that is not a bare pronoun. A procedure opener
(``here is how to'', \ldots) must be followed by a name, a colon and a body that splits on step delimiters (``then'', ``after that'', ``finally'', ``;'')
into at least two steps. Like the speech directives below, the reading is conservative: a teaching that was not meant costs the user a retraction; a
missed one costs a repetition.

\paragraph{The model reader.}
A model is given the user's turn and the agent's last reply and asked for one JSON object, a teaching or null. Its reply is data, not
authority: it is a teaching only if it parses, names a known kind, has non-empty bounded text in every field, a force that is exactly \textsc{always}
or \textsc{never}, and between two and twenty steps. The model reader is asked only when the plain reader finds nothing, and its output goes through the same confirmation as the plain
reader's.

\paragraph{The ledger.}
A ledger keeps entries $(\mathrm{id},t,\mathrm{turn},h)$ where $\mathrm{id}$ is the digest of the canonical form of the teaching $t$ and $h$ what was heard.
\emph{Learning} $t$: (i) it is idempotent, so teaching the same thing twice keeps one entry and re-teaching a retracted one reactivates it;
(ii) a fact withdraws every active fact with the same subject (compared without case), so the newest statement about a subject is the only one told; rules and
procedures never withdraw one another. \emph{Retracting} an entry removes it from the active set without erasing its history, and is refused for an entry that is not active;
retracting the last removes the most recently kept active entry. A retraction does not restore a fact it had replaced.

\paragraph{What the agent is told.}
At a turn where the user said $q$, the agent is told every active rule, and the at most $k$ active facts and procedures that share the most content words with $q$ (words of
more than two letters, minus common function words, with a plural ``s'' removed; ties go to the more recent), as a block appended to its system turn. A turn that none of these bear on is told nothing.
The block says to treat facts as true and to follow rules and procedures, and to say so if one conflicts with what the agent was told earlier.

\paragraph{Confirmation.}
A turn that reads as a teaching is not sent to the model as a question. The agent says back what it understood (``So the build server is called atlas. Is that right?'');
the teaching is kept only if the next turn is a plain yes. A plain no drops it. Any other turn drops it and is treated as an ordinary turn, so a question never has to be answered by a pending one.
A plain ``forget that'' retracts the last teaching.

\section{Speech lessons: a formal account}\label{sec:formalism}

Definitions follow the implementation (\code{core::speech\_lesson}); each statement below is the
specification of a test in that module. This layer is about how the agent speaks, and is kept apart from teachings.

\paragraph{Directives.}
A transcript $u$ is read to at most one directive. Let $\operatorname{norm}(u)$ be $u$ with leading and
trailing white space removed and letters lower-cased. $u$ is an \emph{imitation} if $\operatorname{norm}(u)$
begins with one of the openers ``talk as follows'', ``speak as follows'', ``say it like this'', ``say this
like this'', ``say it as follows''; its example is the remainder of $u$ after the opener, with leading
separators (colon, comma, full stop, semicolon, hyphen, space) removed, and it must be non-empty. $u$ is a
\emph{respelling} if it begins with ``pronounce '' or ``say '' and the remainder contains a first ``\,as\,'' that
splits it into a word $w$ and a spelling $s$ (quotes and a trailing full stop or exclamation mark removed),
both non-empty and neither containing a space. Any other $u$ is not a directive. The reading is
deliberately conservative: a false lesson is worse than a missed one, and a missed one costs the user
a repetition.

\paragraph{Lessons and objectives.}
A lesson is a record $(h, e, o, \pi)$: what was heard $h$ (kept as heard), an exemplar $e$, an objective
$o\in\{\textsc{Lexicon},\textsc{InContext},\textsc{Supervised},\textsc{Preference}\}$ and the portrayal $\pi$ of the voice it
applies to, which is never blank. A respelling $(w,s)$ yields the single lesson $(h, (w{\to}s), \textsc{Lexicon}, \pi)$.
An imitation with example text $x$ yields, only when the user's example was recorded (digest $d$ of its audio), the
three lessons $(h,(d,x),o,\pi)$ for $o\in\{\textsc{InContext},\textsc{Supervised},\textsc{Preference}\}$;
without the recording it yields none and says why, because the words alone do not carry how they were said.
A lesson names audio by digest and never holds it.

\paragraph{Lexicon.}
A lexicon $L$ maps lower-cased words to spellings; \emph{teach}$(w,s)$ sets $L(\operatorname{lower}(w))=s$, so a later lesson for
a word replaces an earlier one. Applying $L$ to a text $t$ splits $t$ into maximal runs of letters, digits
and apostrophes (words) and everything else; each word $v$ with $\operatorname{lower}(v)\in L$ is replaced by
$L(\operatorname{lower}(v))$, all else is kept, including punctuation and spacing. Consequently:
(i) the empty lexicon is the identity; (ii) application is a single pass, never recursive; (iii) a possessive such as
``Jefferson's'' is a different word from ``Jefferson'' and is untouched; (iv) application changes only the text handed to
the voice, never the text the persona wrote, and never the speaker profile or its portrayal.

\paragraph{Reward and comparison of takes.}
A take has a judged naturalness $n\in[0,1]$ and a word error rate $e\in[0,1]$ (the share of its words lost when it is heard
again). Its reward is $r=n-\lambda e$ with $\lambda=5$ by default: losing a fifth of the words costs more than the most natural
delivery can earn back. For a group of $k\ge2$ takes of one sentence with rewards $r_i$, mean $\bar r$ and standard deviation
$\sigma$, the advantage is $A_i=(r_i-\bar r)/\sigma$, and $A_i=0$ for all $i$ when $\sigma<10^{-6}$ or $k<2$: a tie teaches
nothing. The preference pair of a group is $(\arg\max r,\arg\min r)$ when these differ. Advantages are centred and invariant
to adding a constant to the rewards, so a long sentence is compared with other takes of itself, not with a short one.

\paragraph{Objective for preference.}
For the \textsc{Preference} objective the policy is the synthesizer's first-codebook token distribution, adapted through a low-rank adapter on its
talker, optimised with the clipped group-relative surrogate of \citet{shao2024deepseekmath} with a KL term to the original policy; the remaining codebooks and the codec stay frozen.
The two baselines optimise the same policy on the same takes: imitating the best take of each group, and a direct preference step on the best and worst take of each group \citep{rafailov2023dpo}.
