\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A deployed local model does not know what its users' documents, tools and repositories say, and what
it is taught by hand is lost the next time it is replaced. The \emph{self-learning flywheel} is the
closed loop that Splinter, Brain and Sven implement to address this: take a source, write tasks from
it, have an agent attempt them, keep only what code can verify, project the verified experience into
training data, train an adapter (or every weight), measure on material the learner cannot influence,
and release the result only if it is measurably better without being worse at what it already did.
The released artifact is an ordinary Brain adapter that runs on plain \code{brain serve}; the next
turn starts from it.

\paragraph{The question.} The practical question is operational: \emph{can a model be made
measurably better, repeatedly, by a loop that learns from verified experience, and how would one know
without fooling oneself?} We answer it in two parts. First, what each stage of such a loop must do and
what the code contains for it, so that a turn can add a capability without losing one
(\cref{sec:architecture,sec:intake,sec:admission,sec:verify,sec:dataset,sec:training,sec:gate,sec:substrate,sec:requirements}).
Second, what the loop did when its effect was decided by tests fixed before the runs: on repository
tasks with a stronger teacher (\cref{sec:protocol,sec:coding-results}), on a language's documentation
without any teacher (\cref{sec:documentation}), on a 27B model's own agent experience
(\cref{sec:generations}), and in four registered series of self-taught generations judged by an
independent examiner (\cref{sec:rsi}).

\paragraph{What was found.} On repository tasks the registered confirmatory query \emph{did not show}
the candidate better than its base: on 46 qualification families the candidate reached a mean pass@1
of 23.0 against 20.0 (18 families won, 12 lost, 16 tied; $p=0.18$); what separated was the
supervision, decisive actions against every action with corpus, recipe and seed fixed (26 won, 11
lost, $p=0.010$).\cl{QUAL1} The 27B model's two promoted generations did not beat their base on one
measurement on one stack.\cl{G2703,G2704} Without a teacher, a 4B model gained on probes written
from a raw manual whichever model wrote its training tasks.\cl{SL23} Of the four registered
self-improvement series three did not meet their criterion, and the fourth, RSI4, met it in every
lineage: four independent lineages of Qwen3-4B, each generation retrained from the base on the
interpreter-verified frontier of its own proposals, improved at each of three consecutive generations
on a sealed 889-item Lua exam that an independent examiner held, graded and signed.\cl{RS28,RS29}

\paragraph{Scope.} This paper covers the loop as a whole, from source to release, and the registered
experiments that tested it. Its positive self-improvement result is bounded: one task family
(predicting the output of Lua~5.1 programs), one base model, three generations, LoRA training on the
interpreter's labels, and an exam of items the base could not answer; it makes no claim of transfer to
other tasks or models, nor that the improvement compounds because an improved model is a better
improver (\cref{sec:rsi-scope}). It claims no improvement of any public benchmark score. A mechanism
the code does not contain is stated as missing, and a measurement that was not made is stated as not
made.

\paragraph{How claims are verified.} Every statement about the system or a measurement carries the
id of a row in a claims ledger (\code{data/claims.csv}, rendered as grey bracketed ids). A
\textsc{structure} claim is a fact about the code, a command line, a registration file or the history
of a repository and is re-checked by \code{make verify} against the repositories at pinned commits
that a fresh clone can reach (\cref{sec:repro}); a \textsc{measured} claim is a number that
\code{scripts/build_data.py} reads from the measurements recorded in \code{data/measurements.json},
each of which names its raw record and that record's digest; a \textsc{computed} claim is a number the
same script derives by a stated formula; a \textsc{cited} claim states what a published work does and
points at its bibliography entry. A structure check shows that a definition, constant or message
exists in a file; it does not show that the code behaves accordingly, which only the experiments do.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i)~The division of labour among the three projects and its enforcement (\cref{sec:architecture}), and
the intake, admission, verification and leakage-control funnel with its rejection reasons
(\cref{sec:intake,sec:admission,sec:verify,sec:dataset}).
(ii)~The training, release-gate, rollback and lineage machinery (\cref{sec:training,sec:gate}), and
what one card holds and was measured to do when training a 27B model (\cref{sec:substrate}).
(iii)~The requirements that make the loop work, each tied to its implementation, with the ones not
implemented stated as such (\cref{sec:requirements}).
(iv)~A coding-task protocol fixed before any run and its outcome: a confirmatory query that does not
show the candidate better than its base, and a same-seed supervision contrast that separates
(\cref{sec:protocol,sec:coding-results}), with the recipe for another repository
(\cref{sec:replicate}).
(v)~The loop without a teacher on a 4B model learning from a raw manual, with full fine-tuning on one
card, in which the task writer did not matter (\cref{sec:documentation}).
(vi)~Two generations of a 27B model on its own agent experience that did not hold on one measurement,
and the conditions an exam must meet before its decision means anything (\cref{sec:generations}).
(vii)~Four registered series of self-taught generations with a learner that never reads the exam and
an independent examiner whose protocol is specified fully enough to re-implement, ending in a met
per-lineage criterion, with the failures that located what it needed (\cref{sec:rsi}).
