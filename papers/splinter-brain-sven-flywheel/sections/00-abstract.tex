\begin{abstract}
We study whether a local model can improve over several generations by training on material it
produces itself. We call a series of generations self-taught when each generation's training material
is proposed by the previous generation's model and labelled only by an executable oracle, with no
stronger model and no human answer, and we say it improves when each generation beats the one it was
trained from on a held-out exam. This is iterated self-training with an oracle labeller; we do not test
whether an improved model is a better improver. Four series were registered before their runs and
judged per lineage by a separate examiner program that holds the exam, which the learner never reads,
excludes items that match any trained program, and returns signed aggregate verdicts. In the first
three series no per-lineage criterion was met (two early arms met pooled criteria that cannot tell one
improving model from gains spread over replicates). The fourth met it: in four lineages of Qwen3-4B predicting the output of Lua~5.1
programs, each generation retrained from the base on every program its predecessors proposed and
predicted wrong at least once, with the interpreter's output as the label, beat its predecessor at all three generations by an
exact sign test over 889 items the base never answered when the exam was selected (it answers 12 of them
at the serving tier used), reaching 192 to 241 items. Under the examiner's intervals clustered over the
exam's 25 program templates, generations 1 and 2 improve in every lineage but generation 3 does so in
only one or two of the four, and the training data grows from 300 to 2100 records across generations, a
confound that a registered matched-data control and a fresh-exam re-examination, not yet run, address.
The same loop, built from Splinter (what is learned), Brain (training, serving) and Sven (agents), did not
show a gain where it was registered on repository tasks with a stronger teacher, nor over two generations
of a 27B model on its own agent experience. Every statement carries an id in a claims ledger checked
against pinned code or raw records.
\end{abstract}
